PENETRATION TESTING • FIELD NOTES

A practical introduction to the penetration testing process

From defining the scope to communicating the findings, understand the structured stages behind a responsible security assessment.

A penetration test is a controlled security assessment designed to identify and validate weaknesses in a system, application or network. It uses techniques associated with real-world attacks, but it must be conducted with clear permission, agreed boundaries and a plan for communicating the results.

The value of a penetration test is not measured by the number of tools used or vulnerabilities listed. A useful assessment helps an organisation understand what could go wrong, how significant the risk may be, and what practical steps can reduce that risk.

1. Authorisation and scope come first

Before testing begins, the tester and the system owner should agree in writing on the purpose and boundaries of the engagement. This is often documented in a scope of work or rules of engagement.

The scope should identify the approved systems, applications, IP ranges, testing window, permitted techniques, points of contact and any activities that are expressly prohibited. It should also explain how the tester should respond if an unexpected disruption or exposure occurs.

Keep the assessment authorised.

Only test systems for which you have explicit permission. If ownership or authorisation is unclear, stop and obtain clarification before proceeding.

2. Reconnaissance and information gathering

Reconnaissance is the process of learning about the approved target before attempting to identify weaknesses. Depending on the engagement, it may include reviewing public information, understanding the application’s functionality, identifying exposed services and mapping the system’s attack surface.

Information gathering should remain within the agreed scope. The objective is to build an accurate picture of the target, not to collect information indiscriminately.

  • Confirm the assets and services that are in scope.
  • Identify relevant technologies and externally visible components.
  • Record observations and sources so that findings can be reproduced.

3. Identifying potential weaknesses

With an understanding of the target, the tester can assess it for potential security weaknesses. This may involve configuration reviews, vulnerability scanning, manual testing and examining how different components behave.

Automated tools can help identify areas that deserve attention, but their output should not automatically be treated as confirmed vulnerabilities. Results need to be reviewed, interpreted and prioritised in context.

Look beyond the tool output

A scanner may report a possible issue because a service exposes a particular version or configuration. The tester should determine whether the condition is actually present, whether it is reachable, and what the realistic impact could be. Careful manual analysis helps reduce false positives and produces more useful findings.

4. Validating findings safely

Validation establishes whether a suspected weakness is real and demonstrates its impact without causing unnecessary harm. The level of validation depends on the rules of engagement and the sensitivity of the target.

Testing should use the least intrusive method that provides sufficient evidence. Avoid accessing, changing or exposing real user data unless that activity has been explicitly authorised and carefully controlled. Document the steps taken, the observed result and any limitations.

5. Reporting and remediation

The report is one of the most important outputs of a penetration test. It turns technical observations into information that system owners and decision-makers can act on.

A clear finding usually includes:

  • Title and severity: a concise description and a reasoned risk rating.
  • Affected asset: the system, endpoint or component involved.
  • Evidence: relevant observations that support the finding.
  • Impact: what an attacker could realistically achieve.
  • Remediation: practical steps to address or reduce the risk.

After remediation, a retest can help determine whether the reported issue has been resolved and whether the change has introduced any new problems.

Conclusion

Penetration testing is a structured process that combines technical investigation with judgement, communication and responsibility. Strong assessments begin with clear authorisation, stay within scope, validate evidence carefully and finish with actionable reporting.

For anyone learning the discipline, practise in purpose-built labs and environments where you have permission to test. Build the habit of documenting what you do and, just as importantly, why you do it.
